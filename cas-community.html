How It Works

🏆 How the Driver of the Day is determined

The Driver of the Day is worked out automatically from the race log after the race — and deliberately not simply the race winner. It rewards the best drive, not the best starting position.

Four weighted criteria feed into the score:

  • Positions gained (start → finish) — highest weight (40 %)
  • Recovery / comeback — how far a driver fought back from their lowest point in the race (20 %)
  • On-track overtakes (25 %)
  • Clean racing — the fewer incidents, the better (15 %)

Each criterion is normalised across the whole field (from the weakest to the strongest value), then weighted and summed. The driver with the highest total wins.

The clever part: a clean lights-to-flag win from pole scores almost nothing on “positions gained”, “recovery” and “overtakes” — so the award almost always goes to whoever worked their way up through the field, not the race winner. The winner can still take it, but only if they truly earned it (e.g. a win from the back of the grid).

📋 Full race report

Setting

Round 4 brought the Porsche Cup back to Germany and to the halfway point of the season — four rounds down, four to go. The Motorsport Arena Oschersleben is a 3.7 km, 14-corner circuit in Saxony-Anhalt, and it turned out to be a place where the racing was close and the passing was very hard. Two features decided the evening: the chicane, the one spot where a driver could genuinely get alongside, so nearly every overtake of the night started there; and the fast right-hander immediately after it, which collected car after car as drivers lost the rear on its exit. Fifteen drivers started, up from ten at Oran Park. Dry and clear, around 21 °C, 18 laps per race.

Qualifying

Lone Qualifying, three laps each, and Maurice Becker was in a class of his own: 1:24.244, six tenths clear of Willi Brand (1:24.845). Then Benjamin Schlosser (1:25.139), Andre Brechmann (1:25.236), Dominique Meyers (1:25.262), Remo Grossenbacher (1:25.416) and René Hollerung (1:25.507). Christian Schlosser set no time and started fifteenth.

Race 1 — Heat, 18 laps, standing start

Becker held the inside line into turn 1 against Brand's move around the outside, and that settled it: all 18 laps led, fastest lap of the race, 7.428 s in hand. It was a deliberately controlled drive and the booth read it as a direct answer to Oran Park, where he had thrown away both races. Brand followed him home on one incident point; Grossenbacher came from sixth to third and Hollerung from seventh to fourth. The real racing was in the midfield, and it produced the duel of the night: Jan Tobias Schrader against Antonio Cursio over eighth, lap after lap, with the clearly quicker car behind. Schrader was alongside at the chicane again and again and Cursio shut the door every time — firmly enough that the commentary called it hard, at moments too hard. Five hundredths separated them at the flag. Manfred Baar produced the recovery of the race, four places down on lap 1 and briefly last, then back to eighth. Behind, the circuit took its toll: Christian Schlosser spun after contact and lost eight minutes in the pits, Danny Platzer took a 24.7 s repair and finished a lap down on 13 incident points, and Benjamin Schlosser put it in the wall late but was still classified seventh.

Race 1 top five: Becker, Brand, Grossenbacher, Hollerung, Brechmann. Fastest lap: Maurice Becker, 1:24.311.

Race 2 — Feature, 18 laps, reverse-grid top 8

The inversion put Manfred Baar on pole and dropped Becker to eighth — and Baar jumped the start. The black flag came within seconds of the green and the drive-through ended his race as a contest before lap 1 was out; Dominique Meyers was black-flagged in the same moment and served on lap 1 too. On that same opening lap Baar and Antonio Cursio came together and Cursio went head-on into the wall, needing an 85-second repair. Benjamin Schlosser led the opening third from reverse-grid second and looked completely secure doing it — then lost the rear with nobody near him and handed the lead to Remo Grossenbacher. What settled second was a piece of team discipline: Hollerung, Grossenbacher's Big Point Racing team-mate, saw the Swiss was quicker and waved him through rather than hold him up. Grossenbacher was nine seconds clear by the flag. Hollerung then defended second from Maurice Becker superbly for lap after lap — Becker visibly quicker in several corners and still unable to find a way by — until Hollerung ran wide and spun himself out of it with minutes to go. Klaus Oberländer destroyed his car at the corner after the chicane and was out on lap 12; Christian Schlosser retired after 13.

Race 2 top five: Grossenbacher, Becker, Hollerung, Brand, Brechmann. Fastest lap: Maurice Becker, 1:24.386. Driver of the Day (round): Mario A Berger, merit 0.965.

Championship after Round 4

Remo Grossenbacher has taken the lead on 250 points from Alex Gründel (205), who did not start at Oschersleben — exactly the situation the season's single drop result exists to absorb. Then Dominique Meyers (182), Maurice Becker (172), Willi Brand (159) and René Hollerung (138). Four rounds gone, four to come.

Post-race: Jan Tobias Schrader

Schrader came on stream and cleared up the moment that had puzzled the booth — the point in the Feature where he suddenly let a whole group past. It was not a slowdown penalty: „Nein, hatte ich nicht. Ich bekam ein bisschen Rutschen ins Dreck. Ich hab den Wagen aber gefangen. Und dann sind die an mir vorbeigefahren.“ On his long Heat duel with Cursio: „Ich war ein gutes Stück schneller, aber rankommen und vorbeifahren sind zwei verschiedene Paar Schuhe, und ich wollte mich natürlich nicht irgendwo vorbeidrücken.“ He describes himself as a defensive racer — „wenn es nicht geht, geht nicht“ — and put his own result down to not having practised the circuit.

Notes from the booth

  • The field grew back to fifteen after the ten-car low at Oran Park.
  • Oschersleben can be rented in real life — roughly €50 for twenty minutes in your own car, floated on air as an idea for a future community outing.
  • Calapex got a mention: Maurice Becker's new team-organisation platform, together with the write-up now on this site under Software — with the explicit note that the site is new and still being built out.
  • The IEC three-hour multiclass series is still open for entries, solo drivers included.
  • Next round: Donington Park, England, Thursday 10 September 2026.

📋 Full race report

Setting

Round 3 took the Porsche Community Cup to the other side of the world — and to a circuit that no longer exists. Oran Park Raceway, in the south-west of Sydney, closed in 2010 and was swallowed by housing development; the 2.6 km Grand Prix layout survives only in iRacing. Its signature is the figure-eight bridge crossover, but the evening was decided by the corners at either end of the lap: the Turn 1–2 combination after the long straight, and the tight chicane before the final corner, where the kerb lifts a Cup car and the next corner collects it. The entry was the smallest of the season — ten drivers, down from sixteen at Jerez and nineteen at the Nürburgring — which the booth put down to the summer holidays and to internet problems reported in the Porsche Cup chat. Both races ran in the dark: the sim clock set the Heat at 17:20 and the Feature at 18:15 in early-May Australia, dry throughout, 21.3 °C falling to 20.4 °C.

Qualifying

Lone Qualifying, three laps each. Maurice Becker held provisional pole for most of the session on ~5000 iRating — and lost it on the very last lap to Remo Grossenbacher, 1:04.062 to 1:04.136. Benedikt Barth (1:04.465) and Alex Gründel (1:04.476) shared row two, from Andre Brechmann, Dominique Meyers, Klaus Oberländer, Antonio Cursio, Danny Platzer and Jürgen Michael Kraft.

Race 1 — 24 laps, standing start

Grossenbacher led every lap. Becker settled into second and stayed within about a second for fourteen laps without finding a way past — Oran Park’s only real passing place is the end of the long straight, and the tow is not strong enough to finish the job. He set the fastest lap of the race, 1:03.617, and then threw it away: two heavy shunts in quick succession, a 34.7 s repair and retirement on 16 laps. „Für mich ist Blackout dumm gelaufen“, he typed into the chat. Behind, the circuit kept collecting people. Alex Gründel was lifted over a kerb on lap 2 and fell to ninth, then fought his way back to fifth past a superbly defensive Antonio Cursio — who had earlier spun himself out on the exit of a pass he had already completed. Andre Brechmann lost the car before the ten-minute mark and was finished off by a 340 s stop on lap 14. Through all of it, Klaus Oberländer simply stayed on the road: seventh to second, 15.474 s down, one incident point.

Race 1 top five: Grossenbacher, Oberländer, Meyers, Barth, Gründel. Fastest lap: Maurice Becker, 1:03.617.

Race 2 — 24 laps, reverse-grid top 8

The inversion put Kraft on pole and dropped Grossenbacher to eighth — and lasted exactly two laps. Meyers led out of the first corner sequence, Gründel was past by lap 2, and Grossenbacher had gone from eighth to second in the same time. What followed was the race of the season so far: twenty-two of twenty-four laps with the two of them inside a second and a half, no contact, and 0.225 s between them at the flag. Grossenbacher had one early opening and refused it rather than take the leader out, then watched Turn 1 for a mistake that came twice but never big enough. He took the fastest lap, 1:03.943, on the final lap. Maurice Becker recovered from ninth to third before destroying the car at the final chicane for the third time in the evening; he took a 107 s repair and parked it. Andre Brechmann pitted on lap 1 and never rejoined. CLS records both as DSQ for the Feature. Antonio Cursio finished the night on 19 incident points and a black flag served on lap 17, and Jürgen Michael Kraft — from reverse pole — waved the quicker cars through and later lifted deliberately to stay out of a fight behind him.

Race 2 top five: Gründel, Grossenbacher, Meyers, Barth, Oberländer. Fastest lap: Remo Grossenbacher, 1:03.943. Driver of the Day (round): Klaus Oberländer, merit 0.877.

Post-race: Remo Grossenbacher

The round winner came on stream afterwards. On the track: „Mega, war eine Megastrecke. Man braucht viel Können, man braucht viel Mut, man braucht auch viel Willen — aber ich finde die Kombination Porsche Cup und Oran Park einfach wundervoll.“ On the fight for the win: the start was chaotic but he survived it and was on Gründel almost immediately, had one chance and let it go — „die Alternative wäre gewesen, ich hätte ihn abgeräumt“ — then spent the rest of the race looking for a mistake at Turn 1 that never quite came. „Ansonsten hat er dann einen perfect job gemacht.“

Notes from the booth

  • Ten entries is the season low. Despite it, the verdict on air was „unglaublich viel Action“.
  • Prize money still requires 7 of 8 rounds started, with one drop result — the rule combination Becker’s zero-score round is designed for.
  • Oran Park cannot be traced from public map data because the venue was demolished, so the broadcast’s track map had to be drawn by hand. As a talking point the booth read out the circuit’s official lap record — 1:09.662, credited on air to Alex Davison and set on 13 August 2003, exactly 23 years to the day.
  • Other CAS series: GT3 WCT has four rounds left after Le Mans; the SFL Cup is at Zolder on 19 August with Algarve now closing the season on 14 October; the Combined Cup restarts on 26 August at Okayama with the BMW M2 and a new rule requiring 75 % of the laps across both races to score; the IEC starts on 5 September at Sebring, solo entries allowed.
  • Next round: Oschersleben, Germany, Thursday 27 August 2026.

📋 Full race report

Setting

Round 2 brought the Porsche Community Cup home to Germany — though, as the commentary was quick to point out, the Nürburgring’s worldwide reputation rests on the Nordschleife rather than the Grand-Prix loop raced here. The GP circuit has its own character all the same: a tight, kerb-hungry Arena section, the double-apex Dunlop hairpin, the near-flat Schumacher-S, and a Veedol chicane that turned out to be the single most important corner of the evening. The field had grown from sixteen at Jerez to nineteen, with several season debutants — including Maurice Becker, whose ~5000 iRating made him the fastest man in the room before he had turned a wheel. Conditions were dry: 25 °C track, 18.8 °C air, light wind.

Qualifying

Ten minutes, three laps, fixed setup — and a pole margin of three thousandths. Becker took it with a 1:56.677 found in the final chicane on his last run, from Alex Gründel’s 1:56.680, with Dominique Meyers (1:56.924), Benjamin Schlosser (1:57.169) and Willi Brand (1:57.324) next. René Hollerung set no time and started last.

Race 1 — 13 laps, standing start

Gründel won the start and led into the Arena, and what followed was the race of the night: ten laps of Becker sitting between 0.18 s and 0.52 s behind, probing the inside at the end of the long straight, without a single touch between them — „sehr, sehr fair“, as the booth put it. The break came in the Veedol chicane, where a small Gründel error let Becker through; from there he left, winning by +6.55 s with the fastest lap (1:56.730) on lap 9. The opening lap wrecked several races behind: Benjamin Schlosser spun from fourth entirely unaided and again later, ending twelfth; Don Utz, Max Coldron and René Hollerung all took repairs, Hollerung losing 2:32 and a lap. Remo Grossenbacher climbed from sixth to the podium, while Meyers and Brand finished fourth and fifth split by 0.003 s — the same margin as pole. Christian Schlosser had the roughest night: a 4:07 repair on lap 7, a late black flag, 19th and two laps down.

Race 1 top five: Becker, Gründel, Grossenbacher, Meyers, Brand. Fastest lap: Maurice Becker, 1:56.730. Driver of the Day: Benjamin Schlosser.

Race 2 — 13 laps, reverse-grid top 8

The inversion put Jan Tobias Schrader on pole and Becker back to eighth. Schrader lost the lead almost immediately to Sean Pfennig, Gründel was up to fourth before the end of lap 1, and Becker worked forward more deliberately before clearing Schrader and disappearing — the final chicane again the decisive corner. Two starts, two wins, on debut, this time by +4.55 s. The drive of the night came from the back: René Hollerung, outside the reversed eight, started 18th and finished 7th for eleven places and eleven overtakes. Gründel closed to within 0.48 s of Grossenbacher in the fight for second but chose the championship over the risk; Willi Brand took the fastest lap (1:57.054) in fourth. Not covered on the broadcast, and taken from timing only: Dominique Meyers drew a black-flag pass-through seconds after the green, served it on lap 1 and still recovered to eighth; Klaus Oberländer dropped out after one lap and Clemens Müller-Rechner after five, both disconnected; Antonio Cursio collected a black flag, a 2:39 repair and 20 incident points on his way to 17th.

Race 2 top five: Becker, Grossenbacher, Gründel, Brand, Pfennig. Fastest lap: Willi Brand, 1:57.054. Driver of the Day: René Hollerung.

Setting

The Streets of St. Petersburg — 2.9 km, fourteen corners, a combined street-and-airfield course on the Florida coast that has hosted racing since 1985. Two things shaped the evening before a wheel turned: the heat, with the surface climbing from roughly 44 °C to 57.8 °C by the flag, and the pit exit, flagged as a hazard on air before the start and then the direct cause of two drivers’ races ending. Elias Schleich set the AM benchmark at 1:11.559 and it was quick enough for fifth on the combined grid — a Mercedes among the Pro runners, and the booth’s verdict was that he belongs in Pro next season. 🎤 Post-Race Briefing — Thruxton R11

The community Wednesday debrief — the concertina start, a frantic lap 1, a 50/50 around the outside, lapping etiquette, and first verdicts on the Ferrari

The Start Problem

The concertina effect — and a fix for next season

The longest topic of the night. Through the Allard chicane the field stretched like an accordion — three-plus car lengths between cars, everyone at the back braced for sudden braking ahead. The proposal on the table: race control announces a fixed grid-formation point well before the start — from there a steady, reduced pace and no more tyre-weaving, so the whole field can compress before green. It goes into the improvement proposals for next season.

Lap-1 Lessons

Cold track, hot brains

A pit-entry mix-up at the pit wall, an optimistic chicane dive from very far back, and a green-flag track the room agreed was “more than slippery” — lap 1 at Thruxton punished every assumption. The recurring lesson: on the opening lap, brake on your own references, not on the car ahead — and assume everyone around you has less grip than they think.

Lapping Etiquette

Stay on your line — predictability wins

A slower driver asked the front-runners for a little patience mid-corner; the front-runners answered with their side: lapping is easiest when the lapped car simply stays on the racing line and lifts on a straight. Well-meant improvisation off-line is what actually causes the scary moments. The room also agreed his space-making is among the most relaxed in the field — the praise was unanimous.

📋 Full briefing notes

The start — the concertina effect through the chicane

The replay made it obvious: by the time the leaders took the green flag, the back of the field had not even cleared the Allard chicane. Gaps of three or more car lengths ran through the whole rear half — partly the chicane’s fault, partly self-protection, because everyone behind feared sudden braking ahead. Two practical findings: from the cockpit the distances feel far closer than they are — most overlays show a gap readout, and around 3 m bumper-to-bumper is a safe formation distance worth calibrating against. And a structural fix was proposed for next season: race control announces a grid-formation point (e.g. mid-way down the preceding straight, or simply via a chat call “grid formation now”), from which everyone holds a fixed reduced pace — around 70–75 km/h was suggested — and tyre-weaving stops, since the weaving is what tears the gaps open. Two-wide through the chicane at that speed is no problem, and the back of the field finally gets a chance to close up before green. Many other leagues run exactly this; it goes into the improvement-proposals channel.

Lap 1 — a pit-entry mix-up and an optimistic dive

The first flashpoint came before Turn 1: one car ran far right along the pit wall — the driver later explained he believed he was heading into the pits — and contact followed. The replay’s onboard raised a second question: after the initial touch the steering stayed turned towards the other car instead of opening up. The room explicitly declined to assume intent — iRacing’s netcode can briefly “glue” two cars together in close contact — but the follow-up steering input was flagged as the avoidable part. Context mattered too: it was the first racing lap on a green, cold track that everyone agreed was more than slippery.

The second flashpoint was the chicane: a dive from very far back, braking at the 50-metre board with a lot of optimism. The subtlety from the replay: the diving car appeared to time its braking off a Porsche making the same move ahead — a move that worked for the Porsche — rather than off its own braking references, and the car behind it expected a two-wide entry, not a late lunge. Full braking from that depth also means understeer; the result was contact and scattered debris. The takeaway was less about blame than about lap-1 calibration: brake on your own marks, and don’t copy the braking point of a different car on different tyres.

The outside-line 50/50

Mid-race, a car on six-lap-fresher tyres went around the outside of a rival who had just left the pits on a cold set — the outside line being, at that corner, essentially the racing line. The inside car had little room left to give under the rules; the outside car carried the speed advantage but also all the classic outside-line risk: the inside car’s understeer was always going to come, and it did. The room’s verdict was a clean 50/50 racing incident — both could have backed out, neither did. The strategic point for an hour-long race: with a big tyre delta, shorting the corner and re-passing with the slipstream into the chicane is often the cheaper move than committing around the outside against a car on cold rubber.

Full speed into the pit-entry queue

A separate incident at the pit entry: a car slowed early through the final chicane to position its pit-in — staying as far right as it could — and the car behind held full speed onto it. The room’s read: everyone knows where the pit entry is and that cars will be slowing there; running flat into that zone is your own risk, and in this case the offending car effectively penalised itself with the damage it took.

Paper cars — damage after the latest patch

One driver finished the race with a bent steering column and a missing splitter after a contact that looked harmless — “the cars are made of paper since the last patch” was the room’s shorthand. The instructive part: the other driver involved never realised the car was wounded. He saw the missing splitter but read the slow pace as a fuel-saving problem — and iRacing’s own contact report claimed no damage at all. The lesson cuts both ways: after any contact, assume the other car may be crippled even if it looks intact, and don’t trust the “0x” in the contact log.

Lapping etiquette — both sides of the blue flag

The most constructive thread of the night. A driver from the slower end of the field described the stress of being hustled mid-corner while already fighting a difficult car on dead tyres: “I’m happy to lift on the straight and let anyone by — but once I’m committed to a corner, I can’t dissolve into thin air.” The faster drivers’ answer was unanimous and practical: the easiest car to lap is one that stays on the racing line and simply lifts where it’s safe — because the line is the one place the approaching car can predict. Well-meant moves off the line to make room are exactly what faster drivers cannot anticipate; one driver recalled nearly wrecking someone years ago doing precisely that. A counter-example from a previous round was raised — a backmarker inserting himself into a 0.3-second fight he had no stake in, nearly collecting a third car — against a positive one from this race: a clearly faster car arriving on a three-way scrap and simply waiting it out. Context decides: early in a race a re-pass attempt is fair game; deep in the field with nothing to gain, patience costs nothing and protects someone else’s race.

Car talk — the Ferrari is a pair of pliers

Two drivers are testing the Ferrari 296 ahead of a possible switch next season, and the verdict so far is affectionate but wary: “a real Giftzange” — sharp, direct, and merciless. It wants calm inputs and one very specific skill: hitting the brake at exactly the right spot and trailing exactly the right percentage into the corner. Get it right and the front end loads up beautifully; let it start sliding and “everything turns to mush” over all four tyres at once. The counter-intuitive trick of the week: where a lift makes it slide wide, holding part-throttle — just over half — pulls it back into line, because lifting drags the rear tyres through engine braking. The Mustang, by contrast, was praised as thoroughly good-natured; and for the smooth-input drivers in the field, the room reckoned the Ferrari might be the faster home.
